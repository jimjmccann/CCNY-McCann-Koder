# ⇒ CAN WE SCRAMBLE THE REPEATS TO FIX BRIDGE NOVELTY? — measurement and literature

Measured 2026-10-06.

The question: the novelty search is picking up the bridges' internal repeats, and there are
well-known ways to scramble a repeat. Does that fix the problem? **The diagnosis is correct, but
scrambling can only fix ONE of the two halves of the novelty criterion, and it is not the half that
is worst.**

## 1. ✅ THE DIAGNOSIS IS CONFIRMED, AND LOCALISED TO TWO REPEATS
⛔ **Observed, script not deposited.** The self-identity scan below was run in the lab's own working
repository; neither it nor `accidental_ctpr_homology.txt`'s scan is part of this archive. The
finding is real and is ours — it is simply **not reproducible from this repository**.

Self-identity scan over periods 20-60 aa on each 145 aa bridge ⇒ **best period 34 aa at 82-85%**.
34 aa is exactly the TPR repeat length. Splitting at 34:

| design | r1/r2 | r1/r3 | r1/r4 | **r2/r3** | r2/r4 | r3/r4 | mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `id85` | 41% | 35% | 9% | **76%** | 29% | 21% | 35.3% |
| `id1104` | 47% | 50% | 9% | **82%** | 18% | 24% | 38.2% |
| `id47` | 44% | 47% | 9% | **79%** | 26% | 26% | 38.7% |

⇒ **The identity is NOT spread over the molecule — it is concentrated in the two INTERNAL repeats
(r2, r3), which are 76-82% identical to each other.** r1 and r4 are the N/C caps and are already
divergent (9% to each other). ⇒ **the scrambling target is r2 and r3, ~68 of 145 residues.**
For contrast `egfr-siteA-id2015` (75 aa, site A) has **no internal repeat** — best period 21 aa at
23.8%. ⇒ **this is a bridge-only problem.** The nine **submitted** site-A rows are unaffected.

## 3. ⇒ WHAT THE LITERATURE SAYS PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO (four distinct things, only two relevant)

### (a) Break the internal symmetry — diversify repeats toward the NATURAL level. **RELEVANT.**
- Natural linear repeat proteins sit at **~25% pairwise identity between their own modules**; the
  Regan consensus designs make **every repeat the same sequence**. Our r2/r3 at 76-82% are in the
  consensus regime, and ~25% is the natural target ⇒ **real headroom exists.**
- Why it is biophysically allowed: repeat-protein folds are maintained **by nearest-neighbour
  interactions between adjacent repeats**, and although the repeating sequences are *"highly
  degenerate, with no position invariant"*, there is *"a consistent pattern of key residues essential
  to the structural integrity of each fold"*. ⇒ **keep the fold-determining positions, vary the rest.**
- Precedent that it works on CTPR specifically: **CTPRrv** variants introduce interfacial mutations to
  break the perfect internal symmetry of the consensus design while keeping it folded; Ising-model
  reprogramming of CTPR unfolding by deliberate asymmetric destabilisation is a related literature.
- **The cost is stability.** Consensus design exists *because* it is hyperstable; moving off
  consensus moves off that optimum. The CTPR series' whole selling point is extreme stability.

### (b) Sequence design at elevated sampling temperature. **RELEVANT, and we already own the tool.**
ProteinMPNN's temperature knob trades sequence recovery for diversity: *"diversity of sequences
generated by MPNN could be considerably increased, with only a very small decrease in average
sequence recovery, by carrying out inference at higher temperatures"*, with **T ≈ 0.5** reported as a
favourable recovery/diversity balance. ⇒ this is a one-flag change on our existing SolubleMPNN path.

### (c) Combinatorial CODON scrambling. **NOT RELEVANT TO NOVELTY — and worth stating, because the
word "scramble" points straight at it.** Nat. Mater. 2016 ("Combinatorial codon scrambling enables
scalable gene synthesis and amplification of repetitive proteins") picks **different synonymous
codons** per repeat so the DNA stops being repetitive — solved as a De Bruijn-graph / TSP-like integer
program. It exists because *"PCR of genes encoding repetitive proteins either fails or generates
nonspecific products."*
**It changes the DNA and leaves the PROTEIN sequence byte-identical.** Novelty is scored on the
protein ⇒ **zero novelty effect.** It IS however directly useful to us for a different reason: our
145 aa bridge with two 76-82%-identical repeats is exactly the class of gene that fails to synthesise
cleanly, and the organizers synthesise what we submit. ⇒ worth knowing, wrong tool for this job.

### (d) Negative / multistate design. Standard framing (*"design against competing alternatives"*),
and there is a 2025 result on *fold reproducibility in computational design of proteins
non-homologous to wild-type sequences* — i.e. low-identity redesign can keep the fold. That
paper's numbers were not retrievable, so no threshold is quoted from it.

## 4. THE CEILING, AND IT IS THE WHOLE ANSWER: SEQUENCE NOVELTY ≠ STRUCTURAL NOVELTY
Level 4 requires **BOTH** halves: identity ≤30% **AND** less than moderate structural similarity.

| half | bridge now | after a perfect scramble |
|---|---|---|
| sequence ≤30% | **59.0-64.2%** FAIL | plausibly PASS — this is what scrambling fixes |
| structure < moderate | **TM 0.967-0.977** (HIGH) | **UNCHANGED** |

**TM ≥ 0.5 is the definition of "same global fold."** A scramble that keeps the protein folded keeps
the fold, so it keeps the TM. The literature states this directly: a study combining network design
with structural refinement *"could create novel protein sequences but the structural folds were
generally close to PDB structures (average TM-score = 0.78)"* — **sequence novelty does not translate
to structural novelty.** Ours is 0.97, far worse than that example.
⇒ **Scrambling moves the bridges from "fails BOTH halves" to "fails ONE half" — i.e. into the same
class as the nine submitted site-A binders. It does NOT make the bridge de novo.** Whether that is worth
anything depends entirely on whether levels 2-3 are graded, which this repo does not know (⇒
`NOVELTY_MEASURED_20261006.md` §5.1). **The only fix for the structural half is a NON-REPEAT bridge
backbone**, which is a design job, not a sequence job.

## 5. ⇒ CONCLUSION, AND WHAT IS WORTH DOING LATER
**Scrambling was not done for this set.** Two reasons, both hard:
1. **A scrambled bridge is a NEW MOLECULE WITH ZERO BINDING EVIDENCE.** `id85`'s entire case is
   sequence-specific measurement — deep label 0.936, `full_both` 20/24. Scrambling r2+r3 touches ~68
   of 145 residues including interface-adjacent positions, so none of that evidence transfers.
   Variants could be generated and folded, but the occupancy measurements that justify the molecule
   could not be rebuilt in time. ⇒ it trades a measured binder for an unmeasured one.
2. It buys at most one of the two halves (§4), for the molecule class whose *other* half is the
   worst in the set.

⇒ The molecules that already **pass the sequence half** without any redesign are `id2` and `id73`,
at 27.5% and 29.5%.

⇒ **Worth doing in any later round, where it is genuinely the right move:**
   a. **Do not build the next bridge on a repeat scaffold** if novelty is scored. A repeat fold
      caps the structural half permanently; pick a non-repeat two-lobe topology instead.
   b. If a repeat scaffold is kept, **design r2/r3 independently** (break the symmetry) toward the
      natural ~25% inter-repeat identity, holding the fold-determining TPR positions fixed, and
      **re-run `scripts/novelty_chainA_foldseek.py` on the variants** — that measurement is cheap
      and now exists, so the sequence-half floor is findable empirically rather than argued.
   c. **Raise SolubleMPNN sampling temperature (T ≈ 0.5)** on bridge arms and keep novelty as a
      * reported column* in the scoring table, not an afterthought at submission time.
   d. **Codon-scramble the gene regardless** when a repeat protein is ordered — synthesis
      insurance, not novelty.
